Prevention: what you can do now

Some risk factors for urinary incontinence can be changed. Simple habits reduce the load on the pelvic floor and help you keep control of your bladder — even if you have no symptoms yet.

Keep a healthy weight

Excess body weight constantly increases pressure on the bladder and pelvic floor muscles. In the study of women in Kazakhstan, a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher was linked to twice the odds of urinary incontinence. Even moderate weight loss often reduces leakage.

Train your pelvic floor

Regular exercises strengthen the muscles that support the bladder and urethra. They are especially useful during pregnancy, after birth and around menopause. See Pelvic floor muscle training for the technique.

Everyday habits

  • Avoid constipation. Straining weakens the pelvic floor. Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, enough fluid and movement all help.
  • Drink enough, but not to extremes. Severely restricting fluids concentrates urine and can increase urgency; drinking too much increases how often you need to go. Aim for pale urine and no thirst.
  • Watch caffeine and alcohol. Coffee, strong tea, energy drinks, fizzy drinks and alcohol make urgency worse for some women.
  • Treat a chronic cough and stop smoking. Frequent coughing means repeated bursts of pressure on the pelvic floor.
  • Lift correctly. Bend your knees, keep the load close to your body and breathe out as you lift instead of holding your breath.
  • Don't go to the toilet "just in case". Frequent trips without an urge train the bladder to empty at smaller volumes.

Early detection

If leakage has started, don't wait for it to get worse. The earlier conservative care begins, the better the result usually is. Take the short ICIQ-SF test and discuss the result with your doctor at your next appointment.

Important

This is general advice. If you have long-term conditions, especially kidney or heart disease, discuss fluid intake and exercise with your doctor.
